1. Problem Statement & Objectives

Electrical utilities require accurate load forecasting to balance grid power generation, prevent blackouts, and manage renewable energy integration. Industrial energy consumption is driven by machine operating shifts, reactive power dynamics, power-factor penalties, and ambient meteorological temperatures.

This project implements a full machine learning pipeline to forecast the active electrical energy consumption (Usage_kWh) of a South Korean steel manufacturing plant from 15-minute sensor readings. Three complementary regressors are compared:

  • Decision Tree Regressor (CART) – captures non-linear, step-function relationships; depth regularised to prevent overfitting.
  • Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) – linear baseline with interpretable coefficients.
  • K-Nearest Neighbours Regressor (KNN) – instance-based, non-parametric approach.
Core Learning Objectives: Non-linear regression surfaces · Decision Tree step-function predictions vs continuous linear models · MSE splitting criterion · Tree depth regularisation · Evaluation metrics (MAE, RMSE, R², MAPE)

4. Implementation Workflow

1
Phase 1 – Temporal Feature Extraction

Parse 15-minute timestamps → extract hour, minute, day_of_week_num, month, and industrial shift (Morning 06–14 / Afternoon 14–22 / Night 22–06). One-hot encode WeekStatus, Day_of_week, Load_Type, and shift labels.

2
Phase 2 – Feature Scaling & Correlation

Compute Pearson correlations of all numeric + temporal features against Usage_kWh. Apply StandardScaler fitted only on the training split (80%) to avoid data leakage. CO₂ (r = 0.988) and Lagging Reactive Power (r = 0.896) are the two dominant predictors.

3
Phase 3 – Regression Tree Training (Depth Sweep 1→20)

Train a DecisionTreeRegressor for every max_depth from 1 to 20. Record training RMSE and 5-fold cross-validation RMSE. Plot both curves to identify the bias–variance trade-off and select the depth that minimises CV RMSE.

4
Phase 4 – Step-Function Response Visualisation

Sort training samples by Lagging Reactive Power and predict with the optimal DT. The resulting curve reveals the piecewise-constant (step-function) response surface. Overlay the MLR linear response on the same axes for direct comparison.

5
Phase 5 – Model Benchmarking (5-Fold CV)

Evaluate all three models (DT at optimal depth, MLR, KNN with k=5) using stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the training partition. Report MAE, RMSE, R², and MAPE for each model. Visualise results with side-by-side bar charts.

5. Machine Learning Algorithms

5.1 Decision Tree Regressor (CART)

CART (Classification and Regression Trees) recursively partitions the feature space using binary splits that minimise the Mean Squared Error (MSE) within each region. Each leaf stores the mean target value of its training samples, producing piecewise-constant predictions.

  • Splitting criterion: MSE minimisation
  • Regularisation: max_depth sweep from 1 to 20 to control overfitting
  • Advantages: Non-linear, no feature scaling required, interpretable rules
  • Disadvantage: Prone to overfitting at large depths without regularisation

5.2 Multiple Linear Regression (MLR)

MLR estimates the best-fit hyperplane using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS). It provides a smooth, continuous prediction surface and interpretable coefficients but cannot capture non-linear interactions.

  • Assumption: Linear relationship between features and target
  • Role here: Linear baseline and MLR response surface comparison

5.3 K-Nearest Neighbours Regressor (KNN)

KNN predicts the target as the mean of the k nearest training points in feature space (Euclidean distance after standardisation). It is non-parametric and adapts locally to the data distribution.

  • Hyper-parameter: k = 5 neighbours (default benchmark)
  • Requirement: Feature standardisation is critical for distance-based methods

8. Key Findings & Conclusions

  • Decision Tree outperforms both MLR and KNN by a wide margin on this dataset (CV RMSE 1.645 vs 4.870 for MLR and 5.276 for KNN), achieving R² = 0.9976 — demonstrating that industrial energy consumption has strong non-linear structure.
  • CO₂ and Lagging Reactive Power are the two dominant predictors with Pearson r = 0.988 and 0.896 respectively, confirming the direct physical relationship between reactive power dynamics and active energy draw.
  • The depth sweep clearly reveals the bias–variance trade-off: training RMSE drops to 0.13 at depth 20 while CV RMSE stabilises around 1.65, showing a train/test gap of ~12×. For more constrained deployments, depth 8–10 offers a better balance (CV RMSE ≈ 2.4).
  • The step-function response surface shows that the DT captures rapid non-linear jumps in consumption at specific reactive-power thresholds — a behaviour that MLR's smooth linear surface cannot replicate.
  • KNN and MLR are competitive with each other (R² ≈ 0.976–0.979), suggesting that once non-linearity is accounted for (KNN adapts locally), the residual variance is comparable to a global linear fit.
